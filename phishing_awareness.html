🎣 Phishing Awareness Training

Learn to spot, avoid and report phishing. About 15 minutes.

1. What is phishing?

Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be someone you trust (a bank, your boss, a delivery company) to trick you into clicking a bad link, opening a file, or giving away passwords or money.

Common types

  • Email phishing: mass emails with fake links or attachments.
  • Spear phishing: a message written for one person, using real details about them.
  • Whaling: spear phishing aimed at executives.
  • Smishing: phishing by SMS or WhatsApp.
  • Vishing: phishing by phone call.
  • Business Email Compromise (BEC): a fake “boss” or “supplier” asks for a payment or bank change.
Most successful attacks start with one person clicking one link.

Subject: URGENT: Your account will be closed in 24 hours!
Dear Customer,
We noticed suspicious activity on your account. Please verify your identity here and enter your password and card number.
Failure to complie will result in permanent suspention.

Flags found: 0 / 7

Quick checklist

  • Check the real sender address, not just the name.
  • Hover over links before clicking.
  • Be wary of urgency, threats and too-good-to-be-true offers.
  • Unexpected attachments (.zip, .exe, .docm, .html) are risky.

  • The domain is the part just before the first single “/”; read it from right to left.
  • A padlock (HTTPS) only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites can have it too.
  • Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark for banking and email.
  • Be careful with login pages reached from a message, QR code or ad.

4. Social engineering tactics

Attackers target human emotions, not computers.

TacticExample
Urgency“Act now or lose access!”
Authority“This is the CEO. Send the files today.”
Fear“Your account was hacked.”
Greed / reward“You won a prize. Claim it.”
Helpfulness / trust“I’m from IT, I need your code to fix it.”
PretextingAn invented story (new employee, delivery problem) to get information
When you feel rushed or emotional, pause. That feeling is the warning sign.

5. Real-world examples

RSA breach (2011)

Employees received a spreadsheet attachment in a phishing email. Opening it installed malware that helped attackers reach sensitive security data.

Fake supplier invoices (2013 to 2015)

A scammer posed as a hardware supplier and sent fake invoices to large technology companies, which paid out more than US$100 million in total before it was uncovered.

Twitter hack (2020)

Attackers phoned employees pretending to be internal IT, got credentials, and took over well-known accounts to run a bitcoin scam.

Lesson: even technology companies were fooled. Awareness matters as much as software.

6. Best practices

  • Stop, look, verify: contact the company using a number or site you already trust.
  • Use a password manager and a unique password for every site.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA), ideally with an app or security key.
  • Never share OTP codes; no real support team needs them.
  • Keep your browser, system and antivirus updated.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT/security team, then delete them.
  • If you clicked: disconnect from the network if you opened a file, change the password, and tell IT immediately. Reporting fast limits the damage.
